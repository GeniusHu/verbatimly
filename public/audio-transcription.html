How to transcribe audio to text in three steps

  1. Drop an audio file. MP3, M4A, WAV, FLAC, OGG or AAC. If you only have a video, drop that instead — your browser extracts the audio track and ignores the pictures. Nothing is uploaded, which is why a 400 MB file is ready in seconds.
  2. The audio is transcribed. A compressed copy goes to our server, runs through a speech model, and comes back as timestamped segments. A 10-minute recording usually finishes before you switch tabs.
  3. You edit and export. Fix a name, label the speakers, then take the transcript as TXT, SRT or VTT. Nothing is kept on our side once the request finishes.

What people transcribe

  • Interviews → text. Journalists, researchers and podcasters. Record on a phone or a Zoom call, drop the file, and get the quotes with timestamps you can go back to. Label the interviewer and the guest so the transcript reads like a conversation instead of a wall of text.
  • Podcasts → text. Publish episodes as blog posts, or pull quotes without scrubbing through audio. An hour-long episode comes back with timestamps you can cite and link to.
  • Meetings → text. Record the call, drop the file, keep decisions and action items in writing. Nobody has to take notes, and nobody argues later about what was agreed.
  • Lectures and talks → text. A one-hour seminar becomes searchable notes. Export SRT and the same recording gets captions for the video version.

Why the audio is processed on your device

Most online transcription services upload the whole recording to a server before anything happens. That means waiting for a large file to travel, and trusting someone else's storage with an interview that may not be yours to hand over. Verbatimly only needs the audio, and the audio can be prepared locally. Your browser decodes the file and compresses the track first, so the upload step takes a second instead of ten minutes, nothing has to be cleaned up afterwards, and the service can stay free for occasional use.

Interviews and meetings: getting the speakers right

Automatic speaker detection is unreliable the moment two people talk over each other, so Verbatimly does something simpler and more honest: you label them. Every line has an S tag — click it to cycle S1, S2 and S3. Once the labels are set, they are written into the SRT and VTT exports as speaker prefixes, which is what most video editors and caption workflows expect. For a two-person interview this takes about a minute, and it is the difference between a usable transcript and a wall of unattributed text.

SRT or VTT? Which one you need

SRT

The safe default. Every video platform, editor and player reads it: YouTube, Premiere, Final Cut, DaVinci, VLC. If you are uploading captions somewhere, start here.

VTT

What the web uses. HTML5 video players take VTT natively, and it supports styling and positioning that SRT does not. Pick VTT if the video lives on your own site.

Both are plain text with the same timestamps. Convert between them by renaming the file and swapping commas for periods — or export both here, they are both free.

Questions people actually ask.

Is audio transcription free?

Yes. Three files a day, up to 30 minutes each, no account required. Every export — TXT, SRT, VTT — and speaker labelling are free. A credit pack is the only paid product: it adds 5 more files that do not expire and raises the limit to 60 minutes per file.

Which audio formats are supported?

MP3, M4A, WAV, FLAC, OGG and AAC. You can also drop a video file — MP4, MOV, MKV, WebM or AVI — and the audio track is extracted automatically.

Is my audio uploaded to a server?

No. Your browser decodes the file and compresses the audio track locally. Only a small audio file leaves your device for transcription, and it is discarded once the transcript comes back.

Can it separate speakers in an interview?

You can label speakers yourself: click the S tag on any line to cycle Speaker 1, 2 or 3. Labels carry into SRT and VTT exports. Automatic detection is not offered.

How long can an audio file be?

Free: up to 30 minutes and 500 MB per file. With a credit pack: up to 60 minutes and 500 MB per file.

Can I export subtitles from audio?

Yes. TXT, SRT and VTT are all free. SRT is what most video editors and YouTube expect; VTT is what HTML5 video players use natively.
